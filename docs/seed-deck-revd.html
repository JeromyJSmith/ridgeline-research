The problem

Every commercial building owes a permanent compliance record. Almost none of them have one.

Fire, water, workplace safety, accessibility, insurance — each is a recurring, legally mandated obligation that generates inspections, deficiencies, signatures and submissions on a fixed calendar. NFPA 25 alone runs weekly through five-year cycles. This is not an annual audit season; it is a continuous stream.

Where the record lives today PDF Emailed inspection reports, scanned tags, a contractor's proprietary portal, a binder in a mechanical room.
Who holds it The vendor The owner rents access to their own compliance history. Change contractors and the record does not follow.
What it is not Queryable No element anchor, no rule provenance, no machine-checkable state. It cannot answer “is this building compliant right now?”

The volume is not theoretical. More than 2.1 million fire-protection inspections were performed in the United States in the first half of 2024 alone — 64% involving fire alarm systems, 52% involving sprinklers.[1] Each one produced a document. Almost none produced data.

Why now

The open standards stack finished landing. Nobody has packaged it for U.S. compliance.

WhenWhat landed
2024IFC 4.3.2 published as ISO 16739-1:2024. The building data schema is now a production international standard, not a working draft.
Jun 2024IDS v1.0 Final. Information requirements became machine-interpretable — a rule can now be checked against a model automatically.
ShippedBCF + COBie. A portable issue layer with element-anchored viewpoints, and an owner-readable asset handover format.
Alpha nowIFC5 / IFCX. Alpha released. A component-based, JSON-native, layered graph — not a file format with a schema bolted on. This is what we build on. IFC 4.3 becomes our compatibility export, not our substrate. See sheet .
Europe built the plumbing Public owners in the Netherlands and Nordics mandate IFC + IDS. IfcOpenShell, IfcLite and the Open AEC Foundation are permissively licensed and already working.
The U.S. mandates it too — upstream GSA requires IFC-compliant models as design-stage deliverables on all public building projects, and the AASHTO Board mandated IFC adoption across all 50 state DOTs.[2]
But nobody owns the downstream 43,096 authorities having jurisdiction, denser code enforcement, an active litigation environment — and no owner-side data contract.[3]

The wedge. The value is not in rebuilding the schema. It is in packaging it into the American compliance workflow that already has a budget — and the mandates now exist on both ends of the pipe, with nothing connecting them.

The architecture bet

IFC 4.3 is a file. IFCX is a graph. We need a graph.

This is the one place we are deliberately ahead of the market, and it is the reason the product is possible at all. Everything on the solution sheet depends on an object keeping its identity across every handoff. On IFC 4.3 you get that by parsing a file and rebuilding a graph — and rebuilding is exactly where identity dies. On IFCX the graph is the format.

Why layering is the whole thing

Our hardest architectural rule is that Ridgeline never silently mutates the author's document. On a monolithic schema you have to invent that discipline and enforce it by convention. IFCX gives it to you in the format: the designer's model is the base layer, and every thing we add is an overlay composed on top of it.

Evidence layer

Signed packets, AHJ submissions, inspection history — regenerates from the graph

Telemetry layer

BACnet, LoRaWAN, QR/NFC readings anchored to the element they monitor

Deficiency layer

Open → assigned → remediated → verified → closed, each transition attributed

Rule-result layer

IDS pass/fail with source edition, clause, effective date, reviewer, jurisdiction
↯  composed at read time — nothing above writes to the layer below  ↯

The author's model

Never mutated. The designer owns this layer and it is byte-identical before and after we touch the project.
What this removesThe translation No parse-then-rebuild step between the standard and our graph. The thing we store is the thing the standard defines.
What it buys usIdentity Stable element identity across re-imports is the hardest problem in the product. A component graph with layered overlays is the data structure that problem wants.
Strategic positionFirst Every vendor in the competitor matrix must eventually adopt IFC5. Production experience on it, before it lands, is the asset an acquirer cannot buy anywhere else.

Yes, it is alpha. We are doing it anyway, and here is the honest shape of the bet. The downside is bounded: our internal model is a component graph no matter what the wire format ends up being called, and IFC 4.3 export stays the compatibility surface throughout — so nothing a customer or an AHJ receives ever depends on IFCX shipping. If the schema shifts, we carry a graph that is ahead of the standard and re-map. If it slips, we lose nothing we would otherwise have had. If it lands and we are already running on it, we are the reference implementation in a category where every incumbent is still parsing STEP files.

What changed from the previous revision. Earlier Ridgeline material treated IFCX as an adapter behind an interface and explicitly kept it off the critical path. That position is withdrawn. The reasoning above — component model, native JSON and especially layered composition — is why. This is a founder-level architectural decision and it should be recorded as one, not discovered later in a diligence call.

The anchor lane, taken apart: what an inspection is, and what the code already lets a machine do.

A 150-key hotel carries ≈1,600 code-inspectable items producing 2,500–3,000 inspect-or-test actions a year across 4–6 trades. Roughly 30% of those hours are deletable under provisions already in force. The constraint is not permission — it is that nobody has built the record.

That fragmentation is the argument for a vendor-neutral record above it.

How the paywall was solved, and what was thrown away.

Seven parallel research lanes, ~450 sourced URLs, 653KB of dossiers. Companion document: Ridgeline — Hotel Life-Safety ITM Research, Rev A.

The unlock. NFPA standards are paywalled and block automated retrieval — but NFPA publishes its committee First and Second Draft reports free and un-gated at docinfofiles.nfpa.org, and those reports reproduce the published prior edition verbatim as the baseline for proposed changes. That yielded primary text for the 2022 and 2025 editions of NFPA 72. Public.Resource.Org hosts full text of standards incorporated by reference into law. Most citations in the companion report are therefore primary, not secondary.

Method — what is fact and what is judgment

External facts (linked above): property counts, category market sizes, litigation filings, regulatory effective dates, disclosed transaction values. These can be checked against the source.

Model judgments (labeled at point of use): the 1.40× seven-lane ACV uplift; the $8,100 authoring-firm ACV and $6,000 reviewer ACV; the $125K healthcare enterprise ACV and $18,000 education portfolio ACV; the 40/45/15 exit probability weights; every figure on the financials sheet. These are assumptions, not findings. Disagree with a multiplier and the arithmetic still holds — each one is shown in its own column so it can be replaced.

Scope discipline. Every market figure in this document is United States only. The standards we build on are international (ISO 16739-1:2024), which is why the product ports — but no international revenue, TAM, SAM or exit value is modelled anywhere. The UK Building Safety Act is cited once, as unsized optionality.

Rule of record. Where any Ridgeline artifact disagrees with the Master Investor Report, the Master Report wins. Rev D supersedes the v9 materials — the ≈$400M realistic enterprise value, the 9.5× Construction-Tech median, the seat-based plugin TAM, the "94× implied return", and the Wisconsin DOT IFC mandate claim are all withdrawn and are not used anywhere in this document. Rev D also withdraws its own first-pass international TAM layer: the market sized here is United States only.

Standing caveats. IFC5 / IFCX is alpha and we build on it deliberately — IFC 4.3 remains the compatibility export throughout, so interoperability never depends on the alpha shipping.3. M24 ARR of $564K is below the median Series A range and is disclosed with its decision rule rather than hidden. No fetched source shows widespread U.S. CMMS consumption of BCF. Colorado HB21-1110's $3,500 fine applies to public entities, not blanket private commercial property.
